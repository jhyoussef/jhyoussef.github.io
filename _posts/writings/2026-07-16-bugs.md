The bugs in my apartment are a challenge. 
The windows don't seal very well, and on those hot-humid summer nights that they see the lights of my room, they creepy-crawl in through the same gaps that make my air conditioner (doing its best) pathetically inefficient. I can't help but cringe at many of them -- their erratic movements, the way they scurry and freeze, the overwhelming association of them with something unclean. I do not want to see silverfish on my sink; I don't want mantidflies slamming into lights; I don't want to look up from bed to see an inch-long centipede, frozen, huddled near the picture rail. They are something to fight, something to remove; this space is *mine*, *my* space they challenge. 

But isn't that kind of odd, for me, of all people, to say? I study evolutionary biology, right? Don't I claim to believe in environmentalism? Don't I say (if you know me, if you've read my applications, if you're reading this in the distant future) that a principal *reason* for studying paleontology is out of respect and love for life long-gone? An academic focus that comes out of grief and sorrow for what has been lost, and a recognition of the validity of their existence? It feels (or has felt) like past animals lived, and strived, and struggled, and the evolution of systems resulting from that is fundamentally interesting, and worth recognition in its own right. Why do I not have such empathy towards these forms of life? They feel like a challenge to my naivety. 

When I interviewed for graduate school, I leaned into this "empathy model" for paleontology. I cited the famous "Triassic Cuddle" -- two buried skeletons, curled up with one another, a cynodont and rhinesuchid temnospondyl amphibian fossilized together in a burrow. Some suggest that the cynodont was hibernating, and the rhinesuchid crawled in to heal, before both were buried by a flood. Some suggest that the latter was only washed into the mammal-relative's den by the flood. The idea of such different animals, before death, sheltering together stuck with me. But it's a difficult line to walk -- to consider the reality of an extinct organism versus what makes a nice narrative. I don't know if an extinct cynodont had a zest for life; I don't know if it experienced fear, or compassion. I don't know if an amphibian would find solace in a "Triassic Cuddle." I wonder, sometimes, if it does a disservice to the study of these organisms to anthropomorphize them into something that feels familiar, simply because they feel *almost* familiar, as fellow vertebrates. It blinds us (me) to the diversity in experience they may have had. 

I said I wanted to study vertebrate paleoecology and macroevolution through a quantitative lens. The response, of course, was that that is a difficult thing to do, given the paucity of the vertebrate fossil record compared to invertebrate collections. If I wanted to study macroevolution, convergence, methods, etc., why not focus on the better data? I gave a few reasons: the funding is better for charismatic fauna, it's easier to garner public support for vertebrates, and they provide a more intuitive basis for scientific communication and art. And, lastly -- *because I like them*. Because they occupy a unique space in ecology, given their size, scale, and morphological diversity. Because they seem to *live.* The professor interviewing me asked, paraphrased, "Don't tell me you're one of those people who doesn't see something as alive unless it has eyes!" 

Me? Never! I keep plants, I thought, I said. Keeping plants was one of those hobbies that emerged over time, here and there, until years later I had over a dozen lining my windowsills and established an apartment garden out back. I consider their needs, turn them towards the light, try to treat them with care and respect. I help struggling pothos leaves escape their sheaths and guide cucumber tendrils to their supports. My plants are *alive* -- they have no eyes, but that does not, in practice, seem to prevent me from treating them as living. So incredibly different, but living. 

I claim not to be a anthropocentrist -- I see life, and the right to life, in most vertebrates, even those with a bad reputation, snakes, bats, etc. But what drives that empathy, I worry, is merely the familiarity. They have eyes, like me, limbs like me -- well, RIP snakes. They forage, they play, their movements maybe *unusual* but not unnatural-feeling. Vertebrates probably isn't the correct term -- it is really a scale, from more distant chordate relatives through fish, amphibians, reptiles, and mammals. Over and over, it is the features I see in ourselves (humans) that draw me to consider these animals -- bringing them into the fold of "us" seems to be the basis of my consideration and empathy. Plants, on the other hand, took such a different approach to life that finding similarities seems impossible. They are not, then, unusual, as there is no 'usual' against which is worth comparing them; they are not unnatural-feeling, when there is no expectation that their natural would resemble ours. Their *difference*, maybe, is what drives my curiosity and care. 

Bugs, though. Bugs. They have eyes, like us, but no pupils, no irises. They have limbs (we even describe them with the same terms: femora and tibiae and metatarsi), but bend them in strange ways. Do they fall into that awkward category of not-quite similar enough to see ourselves in (as, say, even a lizard), and not-quite different enough to respect as alien (re: cephalopods)? I understand this threshold might be different person to person -- on one extreme, a person who values only humans; on the other, a person who values all life, and perhaps even the inanimate, as worth respecting. Why do we justify it where we do? 

There is a joke that, if there is a God, he is a god of beetles -- Coleoptera is claimed to contain over 400,000 described species alone. That says nothing of the diversity of the rest of Insecta, or Arthropoda. They exhibit a phenomenal amount of morphological and ecological diversity -- is it possible to even hold all bugs in that dangerous, uncanny valley of similarity? I don't know. That is another challenge to my framework.

It's difficult to pretend that there isn't a scale of empathy even towards bugs -- culturally, and within just my own perspective. Suppose there are two axes: the perceived "beauty" of the bug, and its potential for harm (harm to us, our interests, our world). Screwflies are harmful and ugly. The emerald ash borer is harmful and beautiful. Earthworms and harvestmen are helpful but ugly, with monarchs and bumblebees both helpful and beautiful. We tned to favor the latter group.

I've found that it is easier to extend my anthropomorphizing habit to some bugs more than others. I have a few jumping spiders that hunt around my apartment, zebras that sit on the edge of my counter and watch as I wash dishes. I can meet their gaze, their tiny pedipalps worrying as we lock eyes. Sometimes, I feel recognized; I feel as if they know I will not hurt them, as they dangle, always watching. Of course I find them endearing -- by chance, by nature of their being visual hunters, *Salticus* can participate in the humanizing act of eye contact, displaying its intelligence in a way I can understand. But when I see a house spider, do I treat them the same way? Of course not. I jump back, trying to calm the idea that every brown spindly spider is a recluse, as I peer across the room for that diagnostic violin -- or maybe it's not even fear of potential venom, just the memory of running into its web. House spiders can build all sorts of complex webs, the arachnids possessing an incredible understanding of space and the environment. But they don't have eyes that look at me, so. Predators or not, out they go.

Obviously, it is harder to bond with creatures associated with threat: I do not, and do not pretend to like, ticks, or female mosquitoes, or German roaches, and I hate the invasive ash borers for the memory of watching dozens of trees near my home die. But why dislike those occasional house centipedes, when they are just helpful predators? Why shudder at silverfish when I coo at isopods? Just because of the chance fact that I did some work on trilobites, and now project that affection onto the superficially-similar? 

It's all complicated, because they are all just trying to live. And when I see a garden click beetle in my kitchen, I try to remember the Kayla Ancrum line,  "I hope nobody kills me, For the crime of being small."

But then again, amoebae are small, and bacteria, and viruses. Do I contemplate my empathy for Ebola? HIV? Where is my compassion for the malarial parasites that themselves are trying to survive? For the microbes that require my own cells' machinery to proliferate? There is a line -- whatever I may claim, in practice, I do not value all of life's aspirations the same. 

I often claim to want to enjoy understanding Earth history through the lens of its systems--its ecology. The emergent structure of an ecosystem is a product of life -- but also death. I try not to think about it in dichotomous terms -- of winners and losers -- but not everything can survive. In fact, its not always a good thing, if too many members of a species survives. I imagine there is a balance -- there has to be -- between justifying my environmentalism and my field of study through obsessive valuing of all life, and disregarding respect for it. It is difficult to try to disentangle myself from my rationale for the subject I am interested in. To truly belief in the equality and proliferation of all life neglects the complexity of the systems they participate in. To mourn the loss of life, and consequently, to want to immortalize it through study, is to project a human relationship with death onto these extinct organisms. I don't need to find familiar features in them to make them worthwhile; likewise, I can be disgusted by a creature without finding it morally negligible. I don't know, fully, what this means in practice. But it may mean letting the house centipedes be.

Life is living, then. On the grand scale of life's existence on our planet, every being that is living today descends from a line that has, through whatever means necessary, persisted. And, in doing so, they participate in the biosphere, participate in the environment with one another, participate in life. It's mind-boggling, the uniqueness of each species, population, individual. Respect, then, may be better than unbounded empathy. Respect, reciprocity, and cooperation. My aim becomes not to save everything, or even the memory of everything, at the expense of myself, but rather to participate, too -- in the system and the history -- without sacrificing my own goals, my own striving, my own living. 

In paleontology, this may mean treating specimens as the unique organisms they are, rather than projecting onto them something they're not. In my apartment, it may mean letting the isopods be with the silverfish, letting the mantidfly hunt as it wishes, but nevertheless patching up the gaps in my apartment. It means I grieve the individual, and advocate for the species, but still kill the brown recluse. Let any harm I cause be quick and with respect. And let me not confuse disgust and the uncanny with moral worth.

Then again, isn't it funny? Does respect matter to the spider? Its still dead.
