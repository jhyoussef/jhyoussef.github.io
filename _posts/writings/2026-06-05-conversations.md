The homeless woman at work would ask me about the universe. How the Earth came to be, and the moon, and the stars. About climate change and the origin of life, about black-holes and dinosaurs. She thinks I am smart -- I would bring my iPad, or a journal, to work sometimes, when it was slow, to have something to do. Later, it became a way to visualize my answers to her questions. Often, though, there would be nothing but the laminated paper used to log the shop's waste -- spoiled fruit, burnt empanadas -- the back of it white and plastic-enough to serve as a makeshift whiteboard for the nearly-dried out markers. She would ask me about processes, systems, and beginnings -- logical steps until we would get to a question I couldn't answer. It was an odd sight, I think. Freezers humming, machines whirring, the bell on the door silent except for the occasional jingle that would ring when the wind pulls just hard enough on the door. The older woman (nameless, for her privacy, not that I had her real name anyways) standing near the register, looking at an outline of an evolutionary coral of human evolution. The cashier, hunched over and scratching out hardly-legible notes and arrows, explaining our species grand history to a woman who has experienced 4x as much as the cashier herself. There, at the counter, between orders and customers, this old woman and ex-student discuss would talk about everything. 

I enjoyed speaking with her, those late shifts. She, in return, would tell me about the 70's, and Chicago, and Hyde Park's history, but that's a post for another time. I was happy she was curious. Happy someone wanted to know. But it was painful - bittersweet in a way difficult to articulate. I did not know if I would get to continue research. Did not know if learning would remain central to my life. Did not know if I would be able to teach. I enjoyed the challenge in describing concepts. How to do so without relying on the crutch of jargon? I enjoyed the improvised diagrams and engaging with the topics now that there were no classes or assignments to be had. But how to explain it all in the brief moments of time between orders? 

We would stop, when a customer would come in. She'd shuffle out for a smoke; I'd make their order. I'd get irrationally mad at customers when they would come in and interrupt - as if this wasn't a restaurant, as if I wasn't the one behind the counter. "Hi, how are you, what would you like, alright just tap your card there," over and over and over. There was a semi-regular, a university professor who would come by in the evenings, ordering the same thing every time and returning a little while later to pick it up. He happened to be a professor I had had as a first-year. He taught the very first class I took -- a humanities gen-ed on classical philosophy that was one of the most engaging I had the pleasure of taking at UChicago. The first time he came in, there was that hesitation of recognition, followed by that semi-awkward conversation always had post-graduation. The implicit question: if you graduated, why are you here, what are you doing? Research, I'd say, even though it really was that in-between, figuring it out period, post-bacc pre-grad. I was, actually, doing research. I was working at the shop to fund staying in the area, but no matter what, it felt dishonest. The job was taking more of my time than research. "What would you like to order?"

I was afraid that it would be different professors who would walk in to order. Professors I wrote essays for, did research with, who wrote my letters of recommendation. I was afraid of confronting my stagnancy. Of seeing someone who might very reasonably ask me what I was working on, what I was doing next. There is so much uncertainty, after graduation, after leaving its educational scaffolding. I felt I might have taken a wrong turn, and wasn't sure I would be able to find my way back. 

Customers like to ask questions, too -- but not all questions are the same. "Is this healthy?" "It depends on what you get." "Will this make me lose weight?" "Depends." "What makes this blue?" We would sell something called Lazy Blue Chia pudding -- we had to make it every other night or so. It was a combination of almond milk, chia seeds, honey, and blue spirulina. 
*Barista*: "Spirulina" 
	*Customer*: "What's that?" 
		*Barista*: "It's a cyanobacteria," (jargon)
			Customer*: "Wait, bacteria? Uh..?"
		*Barista*: "Well, its a blue-green algae, so, it photosynthesizes like a plant" (better)
			*Customer*: "Then why isn't it green?" (good question)
				*Barista*: "This is really just the extracted "blue" pigment, the phycocyanin, and really shouldn't be called spirulina in the first place..."
	*Customer*: "Is it healthy?" (I eventually learned this was always what they were really asking)
		*Barista*: "Its, ah, really just there for color." 
My boss, when someone would ask, would fetch me from the back to answer. Eventually, it began to apply to any question of "what": What is a peanut if not a nut? What is acai? Is it related to other berries? I answered. They didn't want to know what these all were, not really -- just wanted to feel assured about eating it. "Did you want [ingredient] in your order?"

I am not sure if my current path is the correct one. I am not certain the academic *cursus honorum* is strict, but there are expected roads to take. This interim time, post-undergrad, feels almost like a test: asking me what I miss, and what, if anything, I want to do -- do I need it bad enough to keep trying?  I like teaching, I like communicating, I like studying the world. I ache at the thought that the knowledge I worked so hard to build will be relegated to conversations at cash registers or moments between tasks -- that I will read papers in the mornings, skim over headlines from NASA and Nature, but never find my way back to the places that actually produce them.

The professor will come back, as he always does, holding the door for the old woman, who creeps in. It'll be nearing closing time. I pass him his order from the freezer, nod and say goodbye -- not a student, but just another moving piece in the background. 

When the homeless woman asks me, again, about the universe, I worry that she will be the only one who will. That, eventually, she'll be only one I get to answer. 

The old woman sits, gathering her things. I'll sweep. I'll mop. The 'white-board' gets erased -- the waste needs to be recorded.
