Hello! My name is Julia, a recent graduate from the University of Chicago. I am an aspiring researcher and artist based in Hyde Park interested in using quantitative and comparative approaches to answer large-scale questions in vertebrate paleoecology and evolutionary biology. By studying ancient ecosystems through fossils, phylogenetics, and statistical models, I hope to understand how amniote clades and the niches they occupy originate, diversify, and disappear through time. By visualizing them, I also hope to make the distant past feel not-so-distant. 

Interested in paleontology and planetary science, I majored in Geophysical Sciences with a minor in Astrophysics. I tend to understand Earth history as a dynamic system shaped by environment and evolution across time. Much of my current work grows out of that perspective, combining computational and creative approaches to investigate how organisms and ecosystems may have changed alongside long-term environmental variability, and how that history can be reconstructed from limited records and inherited frameworks.

More specifically, I am interested in how vertebrate lineages -- particularly archosaurs and mammals -- explore an ecological space under evolutionary or environmental constraints, and how reliably we can reconstruct those dynamics from incomplete morphological and fossil data. How do we create, interpret, and reuse datasets in phylogenetics and ecological inference? This includes projects ranging from investigating authorial bias in morphological phylogenetic datasets to analyzing dietary language and ecological categorization in modern herbivory literature.  

I increasingly suspect that understanding the relationships between macroevolution and paleoecology will require leveraging the fragmented information dispersed across paleontological literature and transforming it into structured datasets capable of addressing complex questions. Equally, I believe it will be important to make sure such information is as accessible as possible. This site exists to host my research areas, writing, notes, and artwork in one place, as I try to understand the systems I study, the frameworks through which I study them, and what exactly I am trying to do. 

Alongside research, I use writing and visual art to explore paleontology and reconstruction from a more personal perspective. I am interested in how scientific models, images, and narratives shape our understanding of past and present life, and humanity's position within it. My artwork is both a means of improving my understanding through anatomy, research, and reconstruction, and a way to bring long-dead worlds back into view. Art, to me, can do what data often cannot: make the past into something that becomes connected to living world around us, rather than something confined to diagrams, papers, or museum collections. At least, I hope. 

Outside of my work, I am interested in the philosophy of science, visualizing the past, and religious syncretism in antiquity. I also enjoy gardening, rock climbing, and concerts.
